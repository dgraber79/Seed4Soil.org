Start Here

Tell your first Bible story this week

You do not need to attend a training to begin. You do not need to memorize anything word-for-word, and you do not need to be a gifted speaker. You need one story, ten minutes, and one person to tell it to.

Here is the whole path:

  1. Pick one story. If you don't know where to start, try the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37) — a story Jesus told to one person, in answer to one question.
  2. Learn it in about ten minutes using the Ten-Minute Story Learning Method below. It is remembering, not memorization.
  3. Tell it to someone this week — your family at dinner, a friend over coffee, your children at bedtime. Then ask one question: "What caught your attention?" — and listen.

That's it. That is how the Word gets planted. Everything else on this page — the learning steps, the 7 Steps of Storytelling & Dialogue — simply deepens what you just did.

(Children make wonderful first audiences — and they are often the best at asking questions.)

Learning the Story

7 Steps — Learning the Story

1

Choose the story appropriate to the discipleship stage and the listeners' biblical understanding.

2

Learn the story structure by dividing it into memorable parts with a clear beginning, middle, and end.

3

Visualize the setting mentally — identify with and experience the narrative as if you were there.

4

Imagine the action occurring — crowds, movements, emotions, and character interactions.

5

"Use your voice to project the images you've imagined" — with appropriate volume, pace, and mood.

6

Memorize accurately. Avoid adding non-biblical details — oral learners retell exactly what they hear.

7

Practice repeatedly until the delivery becomes natural, matching the cultural context and style of your listeners.

Quick Story Learning

Ten-Minute Story Learning Method

1

Pray, asking God to help you remember, understand, and tell the story accurately and with enthusiasm.

2

Read aloud or listen to the story while following along with imagination and body movement.

3

Close the Bible and retell everything you remember in order — twice, without looking back.

  • Don't require word-for-word accuracy at first — it hinders deep learning.
  • After learning naturally, refine using the Bible's exact language with an accessible translation.
4

Read aloud or listen again with imagination and movement.

5

Close the Bible and retell everything remembered in order — twice through.

6

Practice your story beginning and ending lines so you can begin and end with confidence.

7

Repeat until telling the story comes easily, accurately, and alive.

Storytelling & Dialogue Method

7 Steps — Bible Storytelling & Dialogue

1
Greet Group

Greet the group, make introductions, and connect socially. Use the greeting questions to follow up from the last story (pray if appropriate).

  • "What are you happy or sad about right now?"
  • "How did it go applying the last story — and who did you tell it to?"
  • "May I share a story from God's Word?"
2
Introduce Story

Explain what is needed to understand the story and build interest: difficult words, background information, place names, or important events that preceded the story. Ask God to speak through His Word.

3
Tell Story

Tell the story accurately as recorded — no added opinions, details, or explanations. Use your voice and body to project the feelings and actions of the story. Clearly mark the beginning and end.

4
Story Questions

Ask simple questions answered directly from the story text, moving chronologically from beginning to end — so the group re-tells the story step by step without explanation or interpretation. Be sensitive and encouraging with wrong answers.

5
Volunteer Question

"Who will volunteer to retell the story as he or she remembers it?" Listen while the volunteer retells. Be sensitive and encouraging when details are missed or inaccurate.

6
Observation Questions

Ask open observation questions to help the group discover deeper truth. Listen attentively, reflect questions back to the group, follow interest, and maintain positive dialogue from within the story.

  • "What do you love about the story?"
  • "What do you not understand about the story?"
  • "What do you observe about people?"
  • "What do you observe about God?"
7
Application Questions

Ask questions that help the group consider what action steps they will take — and who they will tell. Give time for silence. Ask for God's Spirit to speak clear responses to His Word.

  • "From what you heard, what are you going to do in your life?"
  • "Who will you tell this story to?"

An Honest Word

You will struggle with these — everyone does

Every storyteller in every training, on every continent, struggles with similar things. They are not signs that you are doing it wrong. They are the normal path of learning. Knowing them in advance takes away most of their power.

  1. No models to imitate. Most of us have never watched anyone teach this way. You are learning a method you have rarely or never seen. Be patient — you are the model now, for the people who will learn it from you.
  2. Fear of silence. After you ask a question, the silence feels unbearable — so we rush to fill it. Don't. The silence is where the Spirit works. Hold it a few seconds longer than feels comfortable.
  3. Thinking your job is to tell people what to do. It isn't. Your job is to plant the Word, ask, and listen. The application belongs to the hearer and the Spirit — not to you.
  4. Thinking you must prepare clever things to say. You don't. The story is enough. God's Word does not need your decoration — it needs your accuracy and your trust.
  5. Religious language instead of real speech. God's Word should sound like real, everyday speech — accessible and alive — not literary or "pulpit" language. Tell the story the way you would tell a neighbor what happened on your street.
  6. Forgetting parts of the story under pressure. This happens to everyone, in every group. It is normal. Breathe. Check your Bible if you need to. Your listeners are receiving the Word, not grading your performance.

The seed does the work. Your job is just to plant it faithfully — struggles and all.
